The death of the mid-range

How 4.4 million shots tell the story of basketball’s 3-point revolution, and why the long two disappeared.

By Charlie Stewart Data: NBA Stats API, via the NBA_Shots_04_25 collection Regular seasons only

This report covers every regular-season NBA field-goal attempt from 2003-04 through 2024-25: 4,441,371 shots by 2,265 players on all 30 franchises across 26,457 games, each one tagged with where on the floor it was taken, how far from the basket, and whether it went in. Over those 22 seasons the league rebuilt its shot selection around one piece of arithmetic. A two-pointer from anywhere between 4 and 20 feet is worth about 0.80 points per attempt; a three from the corner is worth about 1.15. Teams now take 37.4 threes a game, 2.5 times as many as in 2003-04, and the mid-range jumper has fallen from 35.7% of all shots to 9.8%. Three-point accuracy barely moved (35.0% to 36.2%). The gain came from choosing better shots, and offenses now score 1.089 points per shot instead of 0.944.

01 · Volume

Teams take 2.5 times as many threes as they did in 2003-04

In 2003-04 the average team attempted 14.8 threes per game. By 2024-25 it was 37.4. The rise was not steady: the two biggest one-year jumps came in 2016-17 (+2.9 per game) and 2018-19 (+3.0). Those were the years Houston’s threes-and-layups offense peaked and Golden State was winning titles. Threes went from 18.6% of all shots to 42.0%.

Accuracy did not drive this. League 3-point percentage was 35.0% in 2003-04 and 36.2% in 2024-25, and it has never left the range of 35.0% to 37.0%. Players did not suddenly get better at making threes. Teams decided threes were the better bet.

03 · The mid-range

The mid-range jumper fell from 35.7% of all shots to 9.8%

A “mid-range” shot here is any 2-pointer outside the paint: the elbow jumper, the baseline 15-footer, the long two just inside the arc. In 2003-04 it was the most common shot in basketball. The average team took 28.5 per game. By 2024-25 that was down to 8.7, and the mid-range was the least-used zone on the floor.

Almost all of those shots moved behind the arc. Above-the-break threes went from 13.5% to 31.2% of shots and corner threes from 5.0% to 10.8%. Shots at the rim (the restricted area) actually fell slightly, from 31.3% to 27.9%. The rest of the slack was picked up by short shots in the paint (floaters, runners and hooks), which grew from 14.4% to 20.3%.

04 · The math

Every two from 4 to 20 feet is worth about 0.80 points. A 23-foot three is worth 1.15.

This chart explains the whole revolution. Pooling all 4.4 million shots, the make rate drops quickly in the first three feet from the rim, then hardly moves. From 4 feet out to 20 feet it stays between 39.4% and 41.1%, so every one of those shots is worth between 0.79 and 0.82 points. Moving from 10 feet to 20 feet costs almost nothing in accuracy, and moving closer gains almost nothing.

Then the line jumps. At 22–23 feet, the distance of a corner three, shots go in 39% of the time but count for three points, so each one is worth 1.07–1.15 points. A 23-footer is worth 46% more than a 20-footer even though it goes in almost as often. Only shots from 31 feet and deeper fall back below the value of a long two.

05 · Distance

Shot distance went from a bell to a barbell: 42% of shots now come from 22 feet or deeper

In 2003-04, 39.7% of shots came from 8 to 21 feet, a wide hump of jump shots in the middle of the floor. In 2024-25 only 17.6% did. Shots from 22 feet and beyond went from 19.7% to 42.3%. The distribution now has two peaks, one at the rim and one just behind the arc, with a valley in between. The average shot moved from 11.5 to 13.9 feet.

Shots at the rim (0–3 feet) held roughly steady, 31.4% then and 28.0% now. Older seasons record most of those as “0 ft” and newer ones spread them across 0–2 ft, so compare that band as a whole, not foot by foot. Pick any two seasons to compare.

06 · Zone value

The mid-range never caught up, while finishing at the rim got much better

Teams didn’t abandon the mid-range on a hunch. In all 22 seasons, both 3-point zones were worth at least 0.22 points more per shot than the mid-range. Mid-range shots peaked at 0.843 points per shot (2022-23), while the worst season for any 3-point zone was 1.021. The mid-range was the lowest-value zone in 15 of the 22 seasons. In the other seven, only short paint shots were worse.

The biggest change was at the rim. Points per shot in the restricted area rose from 1.164 to 1.327, and the make rate from 58.2% to 66.4%. With shooters spread around the arc, defenders can’t crowd the paint, so the shots that remain at the basket are easier. The corner three stayed the best shot outside the restricted area in every season.

07 · Teams

Houston set the pace, and Boston broke the record with 53.5% of its shots from three

Each row is a franchise, each column a season, and brighter cells mean a bigger share of shots from three. The leaders tell the history. Mike D’Antoni’s “Seven Seconds or Less” Suns led in 2004-05 and 2005-06. Stan Van Gundy’s Orlando Magic, built around Dwight Howard and four shooters, led five straight years (2007-08 to 2011-12). Then Houston led seven straight seasons (2013-14 to 2019-20) and in 2017-18 became the first team in this data to take half its shots from three (50.1%).

The record now belongs to the 2024-25 Boston Celtics at 53.5%. The whole league moved with them. Denver took the smallest share of threes in 2024-25 (35.2%), and that share would have led the league in every season from 2003-04 through 2013-14.

08 · Does it pay?

Teams that shot more threes than their peers scored more per shot

Comparing every team to the league average in its own season removes the league-wide trend and asks a fairer question: did the teams that leaned harder on threes than everyone else that year score more efficiently? Across all 659 team-seasons, yes. The correlation is 0.45. Each 10 percentage points of extra 3-point share went with about 0.038 more points per shot, roughly 3.4 points per game at today’s 89 shots a game.

The quarter of team-seasons that took the most threes relative to their peers averaged +0.024 points per shot above the league. The quarter that took the fewest averaged −0.018. This is a relationship, not proof of cause, since teams with good shooters both take and make more threes. But it is the pattern every front office was looking at.

09 · The shooters

The volume shooter went from rare to routine: 30 players took 300+ threes in 2003-04, and 125 did in 2024-25

High-volume three-point shooting used to be a specialist’s job. In 2003-04, 30 players attempted at least 300 threes and only 4 attempted 500. In 2024-25, 125 players reached 300 and 24 reached 500. The dip in 2011-12 is the lockout-shortened 66-game season.

Stephen Curry led the league in made threes in 8 of the 13 seasons from 2012-13 to 2024-25. His 2015-16 season is still the record: 401 makes in this data (402 officially) on 45.4% shooting. James Harden’s 2018-19 holds the attempts record at 1,028.

    10 · Shot types

    A dunk is worth 1.81 points; a standard jump shot is worth 0.90

    The NBA’s play-by-play describes how each shot was taken, using 69 descriptions that I grouped into nine families. Dunks go in 90.3% of the time and are worth 1.81 points per attempt, twice the value of a standard jump shot (0.90). Layups follow at 1.13. Pull-up and step-back jumpers (1.05 and 1.03) rate higher than ordinary jumpers because so many of them are threes. Floaters (0.93) and fadeaways (0.95) rate only a little above an ordinary jumper.

    One caution: the league added labels like “step back” and “pull-up” over time (none appear before 2007-08, and the labeling changed again around 2015-16), so this section compares shot types across all seasons combined rather than tracking their trends.

    11 · Clutch time

    Shooting gets worse when the game is on the line

    Make rates fall as games go on, from 46.8% in the first quarter to 44.7% in the fourth and 41.5% in overtime. The final two minutes of the fourth quarter and overtime are the hardest. Teams shoot 42.6% there, compared with 46.2% the rest of the game, and points per shot drop from 1.027 to 0.965.

    Part of the reason is tired legs and tighter defense. Part is the shots themselves: in the final two minutes, 36.8% of attempts are threes (vs. 28.5% otherwise) as trailing teams chase points. Shots in the last three seconds of the first three quarters, not counting backcourt heaves, go in just 29.7% of the time.

    12 · Home court

    Home teams shoot better, except in the season the fans went away

    In every season, home teams scored more per shot than visitors, by between 0.017 and 0.031 points (0.023 on average across the other 21 seasons). In 2020-21, when most arenas were empty or capped at a fraction of capacity because of COVID-19, the gap fell to 0.011, the smallest in the data. The home edge in make rate shrank to 0.4 percentage points that season, compared with 0.6 to 1.3 points in every other season.

    The gap returned once fans did, though it has been a bit smaller in recent seasons (0.017–0.022) than in the 2000s.

    About the data

    Where the numbers come from, and how they were computed

    Source

    The shots come from the NBA’s official stats API (the shotchartdetail endpoint), as collected and published by Dom Samangy in the NBA_Shots_04_25 repository on GitHub: one file per regular season from 2003-04 through 2024-25. The files are downloaded by scripts/download_data.py and kept unchanged in data/raw/. All cleaning and calculations are in scripts/process_data.py. Playoff games are not included. I checked league totals against published league averages and player totals against official records. Player season totals match official counts to within one or two shots (for example, Curry’s 2015-16: 401 of 884 here vs. 402 of 886 officially; Harden’s 2018-19: 378 of 1,028 in both).

    What one row is

    One row is one field-goal attempt: a player, his team, the game and date, the season, the quarter and clock, the shot’s x/y location in feet, its distance, the NBA’s zone labels, a description of the shot (“Driving Layup Shot”), whether it was a 2 or a 3, and whether it went in. The raw files hold 4,450,789 rows and 24–26 columns. This is event-level panel data: the time column is the season (22 periods) or game date, and the group column is the team (30 franchises) or player (2,265).

    Rows dropped and fixes made

    • 9,337 backcourt shots dropped (zone “Backcourt”). These are end-of-quarter heaves from past half court, which would distort distance and efficiency. They are kept only for the player 3-point totals in section 9, so those match official counts.
    • 81 rows labeled “No Shot” dropped.
    • That leaves 4,441,371 shots for the analysis.
    • Court coordinates for 2019-20, 2020-21 and 2021-22 rescaled. Those three files store x/y on a different scale. Using x × 10 and y × 10 − 52.5 reproduces the recorded shot distance for all 594,667 of those shots, the same exact match seen in every other season. Distances and zones were already correct; only the shot maps needed this.
    • Games identified by ID + date + teams, because five 2020-21 games share the placeholder ID 22000000.
    • Franchises use their 2024-25 names (the Seattle SuperSonics appear as the Oklahoma City Thunder, the New Jersey Nets as the Brooklyn Nets, the Charlotte Bobcats as the Charlotte Hornets, and the New Orleans Hornets as the Pelicans). Tooltips show the name used that season.
    • 1,573 shots (0.04%) have a zone label that disagrees with the official 2PT/3PT flag. Points always use the official flag.

    How every number is computed

    Field-goal % (make rate)
    made shots ÷ attempts.
    3-point %
    made threes ÷ attempted threes.
    Points per shot
    (2 × made twos + 3 × made threes) ÷ attempts. Free throws are not in this data, so this is points from field goals only.
    Effective FG% (eFG%)
    (made shots + 0.5 × made threes) ÷ attempts, which equals points per shot ÷ 2.
    3-point share
    3-point attempts ÷ all attempts.
    Zone share
    attempts in a zone ÷ all attempts that season. Zones use the NBA’s labels: Restricted area (within 4 ft of the rim), Paint (non-RA), Mid-range (any other 2), Corner 3 (left and right combined) and Above-the-break 3.
    Per game
    attempts ÷ team-games, where team-games = 2 × games in the season (each game has two teams). The dashboard uses each team’s actual games played, split home/away.
    Average distance
    mean of the NBA’s recorded shot distance in feet, capped at 40 ft. Older seasons record most rim shots as 0 ft and newer seasons spread them over 0–2 ft, which accounts for a few tenths of a foot of the increase.
    Shot map hexagons
    shots are grouped into hexagons 1.1 ft across (report) or 1.6 ft (dashboard) by court location. Size = that hexagon’s share of the season’s shots; color = its points per shot. Hexagons with fewer than 25 shots in a season are hidden on the report map. Shots more than 35 ft from the baseline are left off the maps but kept in every other number.
    Shot types
    the 69 NBA descriptions grouped by keyword, checked in this order: Dunk, Tip-in, Layup (incl. finger rolls), Hook, Floater, Step-back jumper, Pull-up jumper, Fadeaway / turnaround, and Other jumper for the rest.
    Relative share / value (section 8)
    a team’s 3-point share (or points per shot) minus the average of all teams in that season. The correlation is Pearson’s r across all 659 team-seasons; the slope is from a least-squares line.
    Game situations (section 11)
    “Final 2:00” = the 4th quarter or overtime with 2:00 or less on the period clock; “2:00–5:00 left” = the same periods with 2:01–5:00 left; “Rest of game” = everything else.
    Home / away
    a shot is a home shot when the shooter’s team abbreviation matches the game’s home team.
    Shooter mix and distance (section 9)
    for each of the 15 top seasons, the share of the player’s 3-point attempts in each shot type (“standard jumper” is the NBA’s plain “Jump Shot” description and its close variants; pull-up and step-back as labeled), and the mean recorded distance of those attempts, backcourt heaves excluded. The animated figures are stylized drawings, not footage or motion capture: each one has the player’s listed height, his team’s colors and that season’s jersey number, stands at his average distance, and adds motion cues from his well-known form (Curry’s quick release, Harden’s left-handed step-back, Klay Thompson’s compact catch-and-shoot, Anthony Edwards’ explosive pull-up) to the shot type being shown.
    Film room (section 9)
    embedded YouTube videos from the official NBA and Golden State Warriors channels, about 8 seconds each, one per player in the top-15 chart, each showing a three. Most are from the same season as the chart; Klay Thompson’s record 14-three game is from 2018-19. They are illustration only; no number on this site comes from them.
    Volume shooters (section 9)
    players with at least 300 (or 500) 3-point attempts in a season, counting all shots including heaves.

    Limits

    Regular season only. No free throws, so points per shot is not the same as points per possession. No score margin, shot clock or defender data, so “clutch” is defined by the game clock alone. The 2011-12, 2019-20 and 2020-21 seasons were shortened (66, 64–75 and 72 games per team). Shares and rates are comparable across seasons; raw totals are not.
